Optimizing Dam Removals: Balancing Ecology and Economy in Lake Erie

Based on the paper: Zheng, P. Q., Hobbs, B. F., & Koonce, J. F. (2009). Optimizing multiple dam removals under multiple objectives: Linking tributary habitat and the Lake Erie ecosystem. Water Resources Research, 45(12). doi:10.1029/2008WR007589

Lake Erie, the shallowest and most productive of the Great Lakes, has faced significant environmental challenges. Decades of human activity, including the construction of thousands of dams on its tributary rivers, have altered the ecosystem, contributing to the decline of native fish populations. These dams often block fish from reaching critical spawning habitats upstream.

Removing dams seems like a straightforward solution to restore these habitats. However, it's not that simple. Dam removal is expensive, and it can have unintended consequences. For instance, while removing a dam might help desirable native fish like Walleye (Sander vitreus) reach their historic spawning grounds, it might also allow invasive species like the parasitic Sea Lamprey (Petromyzon marinus) to expand their range, potentially harming the very ecosystem we're trying to restore.

Furthermore, with thousands of dams in the watershed, many old and potentially obsolete, deciding which dams to remove is a complex portfolio problem. Removing one dam might affect the benefits of removing another upstream or downstream. We need a way to systematically evaluate these choices, considering multiple conflicting objectives: improving ecological health, minimizing costs, and managing invasive species.

This article explores the approach developed by Zheng, Hobbs, and Koonce to tackle this challenge using mathematical modeling and optimization.

Measuring Habitat: Is This Spot Good for Fish?

Before deciding which dams to remove, we need to estimate how much good habitat would become accessible. Ecologists use concepts like the Habitat Suitability Index (HSI) to quantify how suitable a particular river reach is for a specific species and life stage (like spawning). HSI typically ranges from 0 (unsuitable) to 1 (perfectly suitable) and is often calculated based on physical characteristics like water depth, velocity, and substrate (the river bottom material).

Different species have different preferences. Walleye, for example, prefer specific ranges of depth and velocity over gravel or rubble substrates for spawning. Sea lamprey larvae (called ammocoetes), on the other hand, prefer silty, low-velocity areas.

Combining Objectives: What is "Ecosystem Health"?

Improving habitat is a means to an end. The ultimate goal is to improve the overall health of the Lake Erie ecosystem. But "health" is multifaceted. The researchers, working with fishery managers, identified several key criteria ($x_i$) to represent different aspects of ecological and socioeconomic health (see Figure 1 in the paper):

  • $x_1$: Walleye/Percid biomass ratio (Structure)
  • $x_2$: Piscivore/Planktivore biomass ratio (Function)
  • $x_3$: Total fish community productivity
  • $x_4$: Native species/Total species biomass ratio
  • $x_5, x_6$: Walleye biomass and sport harvest (Recreation)
  • $x_7, x_8$: Yellow Perch and Smelt commercial harvest

To combine these diverse criteria into a single measure of value, they used Multicriteria Value Analysis (MVA). The core idea is an additive value function:

$$ V(x_1, ..., x_I) = \sum_{i=1}^{I} W_i V_i(x_i) $$

Where:

  • $V$ is the overall value or "health score".
  • $x_i$ is the level of the $i$-th criterion.
  • $V_i(x_i)$ is a single-criterion value function that translates the raw level of $x_i$ into a score (usually 0 to 1 or 0 to 100), reflecting how desirable that level is. For simplicity, the paper often assumes these are linear.
  • $W_i$ is the weight assigned to the $i$-th criterion, reflecting its relative importance. These weights sum to 1 ($ \sum W_i = 1 $).

How does removing dams affect these lake-wide criteria ($x_i$)? The researchers linked the tributary habitat changes to the lake ecosystem using the complex Lake Erie Ecological Model (LEEM). Conceptually, LEEM takes changes in tributary fish recruitment (like Walleye Young-of-Year, YOY) as input and simulates the cascading effects on the lake's food web and fish populations, outputting the values for $x_1$ through $x_8$. The paper simplifies this link using Impulse Response (IR) functions, assuming a linear relationship for small changes: $x_i = x_i^{base} + IR_{x_i} \Delta YOY^{walleye}$.

The Optimization Model: Choosing the Best Dams

Now we have the pieces: potential habitat gain, a way to value ecosystem health (MVA score), and costs (dam removal + lamprey control). The challenge is to select a portfolio of dams to remove that maximizes the ecosystem health score, without exceeding a given budget.

This is a job for Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP). The core decision for each candidate dam $j$ is binary: remove it (decision variable $d_j = 1$) or keep it ($d_j = 0$).

The simplified optimization model looks like this (based on equations 2-8 in the paper):

Maximize: Ecosystem Health Score ($z_1$)

$$ \max z_1 = \sum_{i=1}^{8} W_i V_i(x_i) $$

Subject to:

  1. Budget Constraint: Total cost ($x_9$) cannot exceed the budget ($B$).
    2. $$ x_9 = \sum_{j \in J} (C_j^{dam} + C_j^{lamprey}) d_j \le B $$
  2. Ecosystem Response: Lake criteria ($x_i$) depend on the base level plus the change due to total increased Walleye YOY recruitment ($\Delta YOY^{walleye}$), which depends on which dams are removed.
    3. $$ x_i = x_i^{base} + IR_{x_i} \Delta YOY^{walleye} \quad \text{for } i=1...8 $$ $$ \Delta YOY^{walleye} = \sum_{j \in J} (\text{Walleye YOY per habitat unit}) \times (\text{Habitat opened by dam } j) \times d_j $$
  3. Logical Constraints: You can't remove an upstream dam ($n$) unless the dam immediately downstream ($j$) is also removed.
    4. $$ d_n \le d_j \quad \text{if dam } n \text{ is directly upstream of } j $$
  4. Binary Decisions: Each dam is either removed or not.
    5. $$ d_j \in \{0, 1\} $$

Exploring the Trade-offs: Cost vs. Health

The MILP doesn't give just one answer. By varying the budget constraint ($B$), the model can trace out an efficient frontier (also called a Pareto front). This curve shows the maximum possible ecosystem health score achievable for any given level of spending. Each point on the curve represents a different optimal portfolio of dam removals.

As the budget increases, achieving further gains becomes more expensive per unit of health improvement.

Sensitivity: What if We Hate Lamprey More?

The MVA weights ($W_i$) and the model formulation reflect a specific set of priorities. What if decision-makers are particularly concerned about the negative impact of Sea Lamprey? The researchers explored this by adding a penalty or a separate objective related to the amount of lamprey habitat opened up.

They introduced a weight ($WL$) representing the importance of minimizing lamprey habitat relative to maximizing the original ecosystem health score ($z_1$).

$$ \text{Maximize } z' = (1 - WL)z_1 - WL \times (\text{Percentage of Lamprey Habitat Opened}) $$

Conclusion: A Tool for Complex Decisions

The work by Zheng, Hobbs, and Koonce provides a powerful framework for thinking about complex environmental restoration problems like dam removal. By explicitly modeling the ecological links (habitat to lake ecosystem), quantifying multiple objectives (MVA), and using optimization (MILP), their approach allows decision-makers to:

  • Identify potentially cost-effective portfolios of dam removals.
  • Understand the trade-offs between economic costs and ecological benefits.
  • Explore how different priorities (e.g., aversion to invasive species) change the preferred strategy.
  • Visualize the consequences of different budget levels.

It's crucial to remember that models like this are screening tools. They rely on assumptions and data that have uncertainties. The specific dams identified by the model require further site-specific study before any actual removal occurs. However, the model provides invaluable insights into the system's dynamics and helps navigate the difficult choices involved in restoring complex ecosystems like Lake Erie.
