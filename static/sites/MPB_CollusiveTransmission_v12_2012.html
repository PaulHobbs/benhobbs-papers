Untangling Tacit Collusion in Electricity Markets

Based on the paper: Liu, A. L., & Hobbs, B. F. Tacit Collusion Games in Pool-Based Electricity Markets under Transmission Constraints.

Electricity powers our modern world, but the markets that deliver it are complex and often far from the simple "supply meets demand" picture taught in introductory economics. Because building power plants is expensive and requires specialized knowledge, electricity generation is often dominated by a small number of large companies. This creates an oligopoly, a market structure where firms' decisions significantly impact each other and the final price consumers pay.

One major concern in such markets is collusion: firms secretly cooperating to act like a monopoly, reducing output and raising prices to increase their collective profits. While explicit agreements (cartels) are illegal, firms might engage in tacit collusion – coordinating their actions without direct communication, often sustained by the understanding that deviating from the cooperative strategy will trigger future punishment from rivals. This paper explores how tacit collusion might work in electricity markets, specifically considering the impact of the physical power grid – the transmission network – on firms' strategic behavior.

The Basics: Competition, Monopoly, and Oligopoly

Imagine a simple market. Buyers want electricity (demand), and sellers generate it (supply). In a perfectly competitive market with many small sellers, no single firm can influence the price. They produce until their marginal cost equals the market price, leading to efficient outcomes (low prices, high quantity).

At the other extreme is a monopoly, where a single seller controls the market. The monopolist restricts output to raise the price, maximizing its own profit but leading to higher prices and less electricity for consumers compared to competition.

An oligopoly lies between these extremes. Firms have market power – the ability to influence prices – but they must consider their rivals' actions. How do they compete? One classic model is the Cournot model, where firms simultaneously choose how much quantity to produce.

One Shot vs. Repeated Games: The Shadow of the Future

Traditional Cournot models often assume firms interact only once (a "static" or "one-shot" game). In this scenario, each firm acts purely in its immediate self-interest, leading to an outcome known as the Nash equilibrium. This equilibrium is typically better for consumers than a monopoly but worse than perfect competition.

However, real electricity markets operate daily. Firms interact repeatedly. This changes everything. In a repeated game, firms can use strategies that depend on past actions. A common strategy to sustain tacit collusion is the grim trigger: "Cooperate (e.g., produce a low quantity to keep prices high) as long as everyone else does. If anyone deviates (e.g., produces more to grab short-term profit), punish them by reverting to the non-cooperative Nash equilibrium forever."

Does this threat work? It depends on how much firms value future profits compared to immediate gains.

The Key: Incentive Compatibility

For tacit collusion to be stable, each firm must find it more profitable to stick to the collusive agreement than to cheat. This leads to the crucial Incentive Compatibility (IC) constraint. Let's break it down:

  • \( \pi_f^C \): Firm \(f\)'s profit per period if everyone colludes.
  • \( \pi_f^D \): Firm \(f\)'s (higher) profit in the single period it deviates/cheats, while others still collude.
  • \( \pi_f^N \): Firm \(f\)'s profit per period in the Nash equilibrium (the punishment phase after cheating).
  • \( \delta \): The discount factor (\(0 < \delta < 1\)). This represents how much firms value future profits. A \( \delta \) close to 1 means the future is highly valued; a \( \delta \) close to 0 means only the present matters. It's related to the interest rate \(r\) and the probability \(p\) the game ends by \( \delta = (1-p)/(1+r) \).

A firm thinks: "If I collude, I get \( \pi_f^C \) every period. The total present value of this infinite stream is \( \pi_f^C / (1-\delta) \)."

"If I cheat this period, I get \( \pi_f^D \) now, but then I get punished and receive \( \pi_f^N \) every period after that. The present value is \( \pi_f^D + \delta \pi_f^N / (1-\delta) \)."

Collusion is sustainable for firm \(f\) if the payoff from colluding is greater than or equal to the payoff from cheating:

$$ \frac{\pi_f^C}{1-\delta} \ge \pi_f^D + \frac{\delta \pi_f^N}{1-\delta} $$

Rearranging this gives the standard form of the IC constraint:

$$ \pi_f^C \ge (1-\delta) \pi_f^D + \delta \pi_f^N $$

This equation says: The per-period profit from collusion must be at least as large as a weighted average of the one-time cheating profit and the subsequent punishment profit. The weight is determined by the discount factor \( \delta \).

  • Higher deviation profit \(\pi^D\) (more temptation to cheat) makes collusion harder to sustain.
  • Higher collusion profit \(\pi^C\) (more benefit from cooperating) makes collusion easier to sustain.
  • Lower punishment profit \(\pi^N\) (harsher punishment) makes collusion easier to sustain.

Adding Reality: The Transmission Network

Electricity markets aren't just abstract quantity decisions. Power flows over a physical network of transmission lines connecting different locations (nodes). This network has limitations:

  1. Physics: Power flow follows Kirchhoff's laws. Injecting power at one point affects flows across the entire network, sometimes in counter-intuitive ways (e.g., loop flows).
  2. Capacity Limits: Lines can only carry so much power before they overheat or become unstable. Congestion occurs when the desired power flow exceeds a line's capacity.

In many modern markets, an Independent System Operator (ISO) manages the grid. The ISO's typical goal is to maximize overall market efficiency (social surplus: consumer benefit minus producer cost) subject to the physical constraints of the network. To do this, the ISO determines:

  • Which generators should produce how much power.
  • How power flows across the network.
  • Locational Marginal Prices (LMPs): The price of electricity at different nodes. LMPs can differ across locations due to transmission congestion. If a line is congested, the price will be higher on the receiving end than the sending end, reflecting the cost of congestion.

This paper incorporates these features. Firms don't just choose a total quantity; they decide how much to generate at specific locations. Their decisions interact with the ISO's dispatch and pricing, which in turn are constrained by the network.

Modeling Collusion with Constraints

The paper extends the Harrington et al. framework. Firms aim to find a collusive generation plan (quantities at each node, \(g_{fi}\)) that maximizes their joint objective (related to the Nash Bargaining solution: \( \max \prod (\pi_f - \pi_f^N) \)) subject to:

  1. Feasibility Constraints: Each firm's generation at each node cannot exceed its capacity there (\(g_{fi} \in X_{fi}\)).
  2. ISO Operation (Implicit): The generation plan \(g\) implies market prices \(p\) and firm profits \(\pi_f(g)\) that result from the ISO optimizing the dispatch considering network constraints. This is modeled as an equilibrium constraint within the firms' problem – making it a complex MPEC.
  3. Incentive Compatibility: The crucial constraint \( \pi_f(g) \ge (1-\delta)\pi_f^D(G_{-f}) + \delta \pi_f^N \) must hold for all firms \(f\). Here, \(\pi_f(g)\) is the collusive profit considering network effects, \(\pi_f^D(G_{-f})\) is the deviation profit (also considering network effects, though simplified in the paper for tractability), and \(\pi_f^N\) is the punishment profit from a static Nash-Cournot game (modeled as a simpler Complementarity Problem in the paper).

The key insight is that transmission constraints change the profits \( \pi_f^C, \pi_f^D, \pi_f^N \). Congestion can isolate markets, potentially increasing the market power of local generators. This might make collusion easier or harder to sustain, depending on how congestion affects the profits in the IC constraint.

Key Findings and Implications

As the simulation based on the paper's example often shows:

  • Moving from Perfect Competition -> Nash-Cournot -> Tacit Collusion generally leads to:
    • Higher prices
    • Lower total generation
    • Higher firm profits
    • Lower consumer surplus (benefit consumers get minus what they pay)
    • Lower overall social surplus (total market efficiency)
  • Impact of Transmission Constraints: The paper's crucial finding, often visible in the simulation, is that transmission constraints can magnify the difference between competitive and collusive outcomes.
    • Under competition, constraints might lower the average price if excess generation capacity gets trapped in low-demand areas.
    • Under collusion, firms can strategically exploit constraints. They might reduce output specifically in ways that create or worsen congestion, allowing them to significantly raise prices in energy-hungry areas that become isolated by the bottlenecks. This can make collusive profits much higher with constraints than without them.
    • Constraints can change whether collusion is sustainable for a given \(\delta\). If constraints significantly boost potential collusive profits (\(\pi^C\)) or deviation profits (\(\pi^D\)), or alter the punishment (\(\pi^N\)), they directly affect the IC constraint. The paper suggests constraints often make collusion easier to sustain or more profitable when sustainable.

This research highlights that simply having multiple firms isn't enough to guarantee competitive outcomes in electricity markets. The physical realities of the transmission network interact strongly with firms' strategic behavior. Market designers and regulators need to consider these interactions carefully, as network limitations can inadvertently create opportunities for firms to exercise market power, especially through tacit collusion.

Conclusion

Modeling tacit collusion in electricity markets is complex, especially when incorporating the intricacies of transmission networks. This paper provides a framework (MPEC) for analyzing this behavior and shows, through examples, that network constraints are not just a technical detail but a critical factor influencing market power and the potential for collusion. Understanding these dynamics is essential for designing electricity markets that are both reliable and reasonably competitive.
