The Challenge: Cleaning Up Electricity While Keeping Costs Down

Climate change is pushing governments worldwide to reduce carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions. One popular policy tool, especially for the electricity sector (a major source of CO₂), is Cap-and-Trade. The government sets a limit, or "cap," on total emissions allowed. Power plants need permits, called "allowances," for every ton of CO₂ they release. They can buy, sell, or trade these allowances among themselves.

This creates a market price for emitting CO₂, incentivizing companies to reduce emissions where it's cheapest. But a crucial question arises: How should the government initially distribute these valuable allowances? Should they be auctioned off to the highest bidder? Given away for free ("grandfathered") based on historical emissions? Or given away based on companies' current actions, like how much power they produce or what kind of power plants they build?

The paper we're exploring digs deep into this last option – "contingent allocation" – where getting free allowances depends on present decisions. While seemingly helpful to industry, the authors argue (and model) that this approach can seriously mess up the market, leading to higher costs and strange investment choices compared to simply auctioning allowances.

Let's explore why.

Power Market 101: Costs, Choices, and Carbon

To understand the impact of allowance allocation, we first need a basic picture of the electricity market. Generators (power companies) decide which types of power plants to build and when to run them, aiming to maximize profit.

Different technologies have different trade-offs:

  • Coal Plants: Relatively cheap fuel, but expensive to build and high CO₂ emissions per unit of energy.
  • Natural Gas Combined Cycle (NGCC): More expensive fuel than coal, cheaper to build, moderate CO₂ emissions. Efficient.
  • Natural Gas Combustion Turbine (NGCT): Similar fuel cost to NGCC, cheapest to build, but less efficient (higher emissions than NGCC for the same energy output). Often used only for peak demand.

Generators face two main costs:

  1. Investment Cost ($F_f$): The annualized cost of building the power plant (per Megawatt of capacity).
  2. Marginal Cost ($MC_{ft}$): The cost to produce one extra Megawatt-hour (MWh) of electricity, mostly fuel cost. This can vary depending on fuel prices.

When a cap-and-trade system is introduced, there's a third crucial cost component: the cost of CO₂ emissions. Each plant type $f$ has an emission rate $E_f$ (tons of CO₂ per MWh). If the market price for an allowance is $p_e$ (dollars per ton), the effective emission cost added to the marginal cost is $p_e \times E_f$.

In a competitive market, generators will ideally dispatch (run) plants in order of their total marginal cost (fuel + emissions), starting with the cheapest, until demand is met. The price consumers pay for energy in a given period ($p_t$) is typically set by the cost of the last (most expensive) generator needed to meet demand.

Generators also make long-term investment decisions. They'll build new capacity if they expect the revenue from selling energy (and potentially capacity payments, $p_c$, designed to ensure enough power plants exist) to cover both their operating costs and their initial investment cost over the plant's lifetime.

Allocation Rules: Who Gets the Permits?

The government needs to get the initial pool of allowances ($E$ total tons) into the hands of generators (or other entities). Let's ignore allowances sold to non-power sectors for simplicity. $E_A$ might be auctioned or grandfathered based on history, leaving $E - E_A$ allowances potentially available for contingent allocation.

Here are the main ways to distribute $E - E_A$:

  1. Auction/Grandfathering (The Benchmark): All $E$ allowances are either sold by the government (auction) or given away based on fixed, unchangeable historical factors (grandfathering). From the generator's perspective making new decisions, they have to buy all allowances needed for operation at price $p_e$. This is generally considered the most economically efficient approach, as it doesn't distort current decisions.
  2. Contingent Allocation (The Paper's Focus): Some or all of the $E - E_A$ allowances are given away based on current or recent actions. The paper examines three specific rules:
    • Rule I: Capacity-Based (Potential Emissions): Free allowances are given based on a plant's installed capacity ($cap_f$), weighted by a factor ($R_f$) representing its potential emissions (e.g., coal gets more allowances per MW than gas). A generator gets $a_f \times cap_f$ free allowances, where $a_f = \alpha R_f$ and $\alpha$ is adjusted so the total allocated equals $E - E_A$. Crucially, the generator gets these allowances regardless of how much the plant actually runs.
    • Rule II: Capacity-Based (Actual Emissions): Similar to Rule I, allowances are tied to capacity ($cap_f$). However, the proportion ($a_f$) given per MW depends on the market-wide average emissions for that technology type based on how much plants of that type actually run. The formula is more complex, but the idea is that the share of free allowances reflects the technology's share of total emissions. From the individual generator's perspective, they still perceive it as getting $a_f \times cap_f$ allowances based mainly on capacity.
    • Rule III: Sales-Based (Output-Based): Free allowances ($a_f$) are given directly in proportion to the actual energy sold ($s_{ft}$) in each period $t$. A generator gets $\sum_t H_t a_f s_{ft}$ free allowances (where $H_t$ is hours in period $t$). Here, running the plant more directly earns more allowances.

Why does this matter? Because getting free allowances is like getting cash. If these free allowances are tied to specific actions (building capacity, producing energy), it effectively acts as a subsidy for that action.

The Distortion: How Free Allowances Change Decisions

Let's think about a generator's profit. Simplifying slightly, their annual profit might look like:

$$ \text{Profit}_f = \underbrace{\sum_t H_t p_t s_{ft}}_{\text{Energy Revenue}} + \underbrace{p_c cap_f}_{\text{Capacity Revenue}} - \underbrace{\sum_t H_t MC_{ft} s_{ft}}_{\text{Operating Cost}} - \underbrace{F_f cap_f}_{\text{Investment Cost}} - \underbrace{p_e \times (\text{Total Emissions}_f - \text{Free Allowances}_f)}_{\text{Net Allowance Cost}} $$

Now, consider how contingent allocation changes the "Net Allowance Cost" term compared to the auction/grandfathering benchmark (where Free Allowances = 0 for new decisions).

Rule I assumes $R_f$ is proportional to $E_f$. Rule III assumes $R_f=1$. The value of free allowances ($a_f$) depends implicitly on $E-E_A$ and decisions of all firms. This simplified view shows the direction of the incentive for a single generator.

As the visualization shows:

  • Rule I (Capacity/Potential): Provides a direct subsidy to the investment cost ($F_f$). The generator gets allowances worth $p_e a_f$ per MW of capacity built, reducing the effective investment cost they perceive. This subsidy is larger for dirtier plants (higher $R_f$). This encourages building capacity, especially dirty capacity, even if it's not run much.
  • Rule II (Capacity/Actual): Also acts primarily as a subsidy on investment cost, similar to Rule I, from the individual firm's viewpoint. The link to market-wide emissions adds complexity but the core incentive is still tied to building capacity.
  • Rule III (Sales): Provides a subsidy to the operating revenue/cost. The generator gets allowances worth $p_e a_f$ for each MWh sold, effectively reducing their marginal cost or increasing their revenue per MWh. This encourages running plants more, but doesn't directly subsidize building them independent of operation.

These subsidies distort decisions away from the true least-cost options (which would be chosen under auctioning). Rule I and II are particularly problematic because they can incentivize building expensive plants that are barely used, just to collect allowances, or skew investment heavily towards higher-emitting technologies if they are granted proportionally more allowances.

Modeling the Long Run: Equilibrium and Inefficiency

The paper uses a sophisticated mathematical framework (Nonlinear Complementarity Problems) to model the long-run equilibrium of the power market under these different rules. This means finding a state where:

  • Generators have built the profit-maximizing amount of capacity for each technology type, assuming free entry (profits driven to zero in the long run).
  • Generators operate their plants optimally in each time period based on market prices and costs (including net allowance costs).
  • Energy prices ($p_t$) clear the market (supply equals demand) in each period.
  • The allowance price ($p_e$) clears the allowance market (total emissions equal the cap $E$, considering any external trading).
  • Capacity prices ($p_c$) clear the capacity market (if one exists).
  • The allocation parameters ($\alpha$ or $a_f$) are consistent with the total allowances available ($E-E_A$) and the resulting market outcome (total capacity or sales).

Solving this complex system allows the authors to simulate how the market might settle under different scenarios and quantify the resulting inefficiencies.

Simulation Results: The Cost of Free Allowances

The paper presents simulations based on realistic (though simplified) cost and demand data for coal, NGCC, and NGCT plants. They compare scenarios with different CO₂ caps (20 Mton/yr - strict, 40 Mton/yr - mild) and vary the percentage of allowances ($E-E_A$) allocated contingently (from 0% - equivalent to auction/grandfathering, to 100% - all non-auctioned allowances given contingently).

Key observations from the simulations (and the interactive chart):

  • Rising Costs: As more allowances are given away contingently (especially under Rules I and II), the total social cost (a measure of inefficiency, representing lost value compared to the ideal outcome) increases dramatically, particularly above 80% contingent allocation. Generation costs and consumer payments also tend to rise compared to the efficient benchmark, despite the "free" allowances.
  • Distorted Investment: Rules I and II strongly incentivize building more capacity, especially coal (which gets a larger implicit subsidy per MW due to higher emissions). This happens even if that capacity isn't needed or used efficiently. Rule III causes much less investment distortion.
  • Allowance Price Inflation: Giving allowances away contingently based on capacity (Rules I/II) increases the demand for allowances (as more capacity is built), driving up the allowance price ($p_e$) compared to the auction scenario.
  • Operational Inefficiency (Dispatch Flip): Under Rule II, as the allowance price gets very high (driven up by the distorted investment), a bizarre outcome can occur: the operating order flips. Natural gas plants (NGCC), despite higher fuel costs, become cheaper to run on the margin than coal plants because the implicit subsidy from free allowances outweighs the fuel cost difference. Coal plants might only run during peak times, while gas runs constantly – the opposite of normal operation! This is reflected in the shifting capacity factors in the chart.
  • Rule I is Worst: The paper shows Rule I (Potential Emissions) causes even larger distortions than Rule II, potentially leading to generators building power plants (like NGCTs) that never even run, simply to collect the capacity-based allowances.
  • Rule III is Better: Allocating based on sales (Rule III) causes significantly less distortion in both investment and operation compared to capacity-based rules. Costs are only slightly higher than the auction benchmark.

The paper also notes that the presence of a capacity market or minimum run constraints on coal plants can slightly alter the magnitude of these distortions, but the fundamental problems with contingent allocation (especially Rules I and II) remain.

Conclusion: Keep It Simple?

The core message of Zhao, Hobbs, and Pang's work is a cautionary tale: while giving away free emission allowances based on current generator actions might seem politically appealing or helpful to industry, it can introduce significant economic inefficiencies.

  • Allocating allowances based on capacity (Rules I and II) is particularly risky, potentially leading to large distortions in investment, inflated costs, higher allowance prices, and even upside-down operational decisions.
  • Allocating based on sales (Rule III) is less distorting but still less efficient than the benchmarks.
  • Auctioning allowances or grandfathering based on fixed historical data avoids these operational and investment distortions, leading to a more economically efficient outcome (achieving the emissions cap at the lowest overall cost).

While the political economy of allowance allocation is complex, this modeling work strongly suggests that contingent allocation rules, especially those tied to capacity, come with hidden costs that can undermine the efficiency goals of cap-and-trade policies. Sometimes, the simplest approach – making polluters pay for their emissions without subsidies tied to their current behavior – is the most effective.
